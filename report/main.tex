\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

When choosing a hotel, people increasingly trust the words of strangers online.
Studies have shown that people are strongly influenced by these reviews; negative information tends to weigh more heavily, while positively framed reviews combined with numerical ratings increase both booking intentions and trust in a hotel \citep{Sparks2011}. 
For this reason, hotels care about their reviews, as these can affect booking intentions.
One concern for both hotels and customers is fake reviews, also known as opinion spam.

Large language models (LLMs) have improved substantially in recent years and excel especially at writing tasks.
Before this, the volume of fake reviews was likely limited by human writing speed, time and energy.
Now, LLMs can generate reviews rapidly, outpacing humans, and deceptive reviews are becoming increasingly common \citep{Ignat2025}.
Platforms such as Amazon have already reported a surge of ChatGPT-generated fake reviews, which reduce consumer trust in online reviews and harm seller performance \citep{Zhao2025}. 
Therefore, automated methods are needed to filter out AI-generated reviews.

Prior work has found that AI-generated fake reviews are more comprehensible, less specific, more mechanical and less empathetic than authentic reviews \citep{Zhao2025}. 
In a recent study, \citet{Ignat2025} collected real hotel reviews from Booking.com and generated fake ones with GPT-4.
However, \citeauthor{Ignat2025} only compared two models, Gaussian Naive Bayes and Random Forests, to a fine-tuned deep learning model.
We expand on their research by comparing a wider range of bag-of-words models.

In this paper, we investigate how well simple bag-of-words models can distinguish AI-generated hotel reviews from human-written reviews.
We use the English reviews from the MAiDE-up dataset\footnote{\url{https://github.com/MichiganNLP/multilingual_reviews_deception}} \citep{Ignat2025}, which contains both positive and negative reviews, written by humans and generated with GPT-4.
We compare five models: Multinomial Naive Bayes, Logistic Regression, a Decision Tree, Random Forest and Gradient Boosting.
These models were chosen because they are computationally efficient and interpretable, which allows us to inspect which words drive their predictions.
We address the following research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Does a discriminative linear model (Logistic Regression) outperform a generative one (Multinomial Naive Bayes)?
  \item Does combining many trees into an ensemble (Random Forest and Gradient Boosting) improve on a single Decision Tree?
  \item Is the Random Forest better at telling fake from genuine reviews when the reviews are positive or when they are negative?
  \item Which words are most predictive for each model, and which of them point towards AI-generated or human-written reviews?
\end{enumerate}
Answering these questions shows whether more complex models are worth their cost for this task, and which linguistic cues give AI-generated reviews away.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows.
\sectionref{sec:data} describes the dataset and \sectionref{sec:preprocessing} the preprocessing steps.
\sectionref{sec:setup} explains the models, the hyperparameter tuning and the statistical test used to compare the models.
\sectionref{sec:results} presents the performance of the models, the results of McNemar's test and the most important features.
Finally, \sectionref{sec:conclusions} summarizes our findings and discusses limitations and future work.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}
This study investigates the detection of fake hotel reviews by distinguishing between human-written reviews and AI generated reviews. 
The dataset thus, contains the two classes 0 (human) and 1 (AI). For this study, the dataset was restricted to English-language reviews to ensure linguistic consistency. 
Further, this restriction facilitates the application of natural language processing techniques and allows the analysis to focus on differences 
between human and AI-generated reviews rather than on variations based on language-specific characteristics. 
However, our findings may not generalize to reviews written in other languages.

The filtered dataset consists of overall 2.000 reviews with 1000 genuine reviews and 1000 AI-generated reviews. 
This balanced class distribution provides an appropriate basis to compare the linguistic characteristics without introducing an initial class imbalance. 
First, the two review columns \texttt{Upside\_Review} and \texttt{Downside\_Review}, were combined into a single column \texttt{Review}. 
This summarizes the positive and negative aspects of each hotel review into a single text field for subsequent analysis.
In addition, the column \texttt{Sentiment} was included, which categorizes each review as either positive or negative. 
The two sentiment categories are thereby equally represented in the dataset. 

To obtain an initial overview of the textual characteristics of the two classes, corresponding word clouds were generated (Figure ~\ref{fig:wordcloud_human}, Figure ~\ref{fig:wordcloud_ai}).

When both classes showed similar patterns, we applied normalization to reduce irrelevant variation and ensure consistent text representation.

\label{sec:preprocessing}

The objective of preprocessing is to transform the original reviews into a consistent representation while preserving the linguistic information.

\subsection{Normalization}
\label{sec:normalization}
Text normalization is the first step in preprocessing and aims to transform the original text into a more consistent format. 
In this study, unicode normalization, accent removal, and conversion to lowercase were applied. 
Additionally, multiple consecutive whitespaces were replaced with a single whitespace. 
These steps reduce variations and ensure that equivalent terms are represented consistently. 
This is particularly useful for text classification, as differences in capitalization or character encoding could otherwise lead to identical terms being treated as separate features.

However, normalization requires careful consideration because certain textual characteristics may provide useful information to distinguish human and AI-generated reviews \cite{Shah2023}. 
Therefore, our preprocessing decisions were guided by an exploratory analysis of the dataset to identify differences between the two classes.
The analysis shows differences in the use of punctuation marks and numbers. Human-written reviews contained more question marks and exclamation marks per 1000 words than AI-generated reviews (0.63 vs. 0.02 and 5.66 vs. 0.85, respectively). 
In contrast, AI-generated reviews contained more commas per 1000 words (49.02 vs. 35.06). 
Differences were also observed in the use of colons and quotation marks. Furthermore, human-written reviews contained substantially more numbers per 1000 words than AI-generated reviews (11.46 vs. 0.83). 
These findings suggest that punctuation and numerical information may capture stylistic differences.

A similar pattern emerged for contractions. Human-written reviews contained 10.22 contractions per 1000 words, compared with 5.57 in AI-generated reviews. 
Thus, contractions may also provide potentially useful information for classification. Based on these findings, we decided to keep numbers, punctuation marks, and contractions during text normalization. 

This approach aims to balance textual consistency with the preservation of features that may help the model distinguish between the two classes. 
Nevertheless, the observed differences are descriptive and do not necessarily imply that these features will improve classification performance.

\subsection{Tokenization}
\label{sec:tokenization}
After normalization, the reviews are split into individual tokens that can be processed by machine learning algorithms. 
Several tokenization strategies exist, ranging from simple whitespace tokenization to more sophisticated subword-based approaches. 
For example, SentencePiece supports Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) and the Unigram Language Model, which learn subword units and do not require whitespace to define fixed word boundaries \cite{Kudo2018}. 
Such approaches are particularly useful for languages such as Chinese and Japanese, where words are not consistently separated by spaces.

In this project, we applied whitespace tokenization, which splits the text at whitespace characters to obtain individual tokens. 
We chose this approach since the dataset was restricted to English reviews, in which spaces generally separate words. 
This makes whitespace tokenization a simple and computationally efficient option. 
However, it does not account for all linguistic complexities, such as contractions or punctuation attached to words. 
The previously applied normalization steps aim to standardize the text and reduce these potential inconsistencies. 
The resulting tokens are then used for the subsequent stop-word removal and stemming steps.

\subsection{Stop-Word Removal}
\label{sec:stop-word-removal}
After tokenization, common English stop words are removed. Stop words are frequently occurring terms that only contribute little information to text classification, including articles, prepositions, and conjunctions. 
Examples involve \textit{the}, \textit{a}, \textit{and}, and \textit{or}. Thereby, their frequent occurrence across documents can increase the size of the feature representation without necessarily improving the model's ability to distinguish between classes.

In this study, the English stop-word list provided by the Natural Language Toolkit (NLTK) is used. 
Removing these words allows the analysis to focus more strongly on terms that may be informative for distinguishing. 
For example, rather than emphasizing frequently occurring grammatical words such as the and and, the resulting representation places greater emphasis on descriptive terms related to hotel rooms, staff, service, and location.

Stop-word removal in general is a common preprocessing technique in text classification, although its effectiveness depends on the task. 
Based on the initial word frequency analysis, frequent function words did not appear to be particularly useful for distinguishing the two classes. 
Therefore, we removed these words to focus on content-related terms that may be more informative for classification. 
However, since common words can also contain stylistic information, the suitability of stop-word removal depends on the specific classification task.

\subsection{Stemming}
\label{sec:stemming}
The next preprocessing step is stemming, which reduces words to a common stem by removing certain prefixes or suffixes. 
This process helps to group related word forms and reduces the number of distinct terms in the vocabulary. 
For example, words such as \textit{connect}, \textit{connected}, and \textit{connecting} may be reduced to a common stem. 
Compared to stop-word removal, stemming generally preserves more of the original textual content, as it changes the form of words rather than removing them entirely. 
This can reduce vocabulary redundancy and improve computational efficiency during model training.

For this study the Porter stemming algorithm is applied, since it is a widely used method designed specifically for English \cite{Willett2006}. 
By consolidating related word forms, stemming reduces lexical variation and can help machine learning models identify recurring patterns across reviews, even when the same concept appears in different grammatical forms.

However, stemming does not necessarily produce linguistically valid words. It applies rule-based transformations to obtain stems rather than complete dictionary forms. 
Furthermore, different words may occasionally be reduced to the same stem despite having different meanings. 
Neverthelsee, we applied stemming in this project to reduce vocabulary size and group related word forms. 
Since the dataset is restricted to English hotel reviews, the vocabulary is relatively focused on a specific domain, which helps to limit the potential drawbacks of stemming. 
Although these limitations cannot be entirely avoided, the domain-specific context makes stemming a reasonable choice for this classification task.

\subsection{Training and Test Split}
\label{sec:train-test-split}
The final step is to divide the preprocessed dataset into training and test subsets. 
The training data is used to fit the classification models, whereas the test data is kept for evaluation. 
We applied stratified splitting to preserve the 50/50 class distribution in both data sets. 
This ensures that both classes are equally represented during training, such that the model can learn patterns from human-written and AI-generated reviews without favoring one class due to different class frequencies. 
A separate validation set is not necessarily required for this experimental setup since we use use cross-validation for the final evaluation. 

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

All models in this experiment used a bag-of-words representation. 
This approach provides a method for representing text data by treating word counts as primary features, although it inherently lacks the ability to capture word order.
Each model was tuned using a grid search with 5-fold cross-validation to identify the optimal hyperparameters (\tableref{tab:hyperparameters}).
5-fold cross-validation was chosen to balance the need for robust evaluation against the limitations of the dataset size and available computational time.

The first model chosen was Multinomial Naive Bayes.
As a standard model for text classification, it counts word frequencies per class.
By beginning with this baseline, we can evaluate whether increased model complexity yields improvements in accuracy.
We used the chi-squared test to select the top $k$ features, which assists in removing features that are less informative for prediction.
Additionally, the minimum document frequency ($\text{min\_df}$) was tuned via grid search to remove sparse terms.
However, the selected $k$ was all features, indicating that feature selection did not improve performance.

The second model selected was Logistic Regression.
This is another common and computationally efficient baseline for text classification, as it calculates a weight for each term within the bag-of-words representation, making the model highly interpretable.
To prevent overfitting given the limited sample size, L1 regularization was applied to penalize large weights.
The regularization strength $C$ (where $C = 1/\lambda$) was tuned over a range of 0.01 to 100.
While Multinomial Naive Bayes uses top $k$ feature selection as a tunable step, L1 regularization removes uninformative terms by setting their weights to zero, which is not possible for Naive Bayes as it has no learned weights to penalize.
As with the previous model, $\text{min\_df}$ was also employed to eliminate sparse terms.

The third model deployed was the Decision Tree.
This model was chosen for its interpretability, allowing for a clear analysis of the factors driving its predictions.
Furthermore, since a Random Forest model is also implemented, a single Decision tree serves as a necessary baseline to determine if an ensemble approach improves performance, as documented in the literature \citep{Breiman2001}.
The Decision Tree Classifier was implemented using the scikit-learn library with the default Gini setting.
However, since the default $\text{ccp\_alpha}$ is 0, we performed a grid search to optimize this hyperparameter.
Without tuning $\text{ccp\_alpha}$ the tree can become overly complex and lead to overfitting.
The final model for testing was refit on the entire training set once the optimal $\alpha$ was found. 



The fourth model was Random Forest \citep{Breiman2001}.
A single Decision Tree has a high variance, since small changes in the training data can lead to a very different tree.
Random Forest reduces this variance by training many trees on bootstrap samples of the training set and letting them vote on the final class.
In addition, each split only considers a random subset of the features, which makes the trees less correlated with each other, so their errors cancel out more when combined.
We tuned $\text{min\_df}$, the number of features considered at each split ($\text{max\_features}$) and the number of trees ($\text{n\_estimators}$).
The trees were grown to full depth, as is common for Random Forest, because averaging over many trees counteracts the overfitting of the individual trees.
The selected $\text{max\_features}$ was $\log_2 p$, which with a vocabulary of $p = 4443$ words means that each split only considers 12 randomly chosen words, resulting in strongly decorrelated trees.
The largest number of trees in the grid (500) was selected, so adding more trees might improve the performance slightly further.

The fifth model was Gradient Boosting \citep{4a848dd1-54e3-3c3c-83c3-04977ded2e71}.
Whereas Random Forest builds independent trees, Gradient Boosting builds trees sequentially, where each new tree is fitted to the errors (the gradient of the log-loss) of the trees before it.
Random Forest mainly reduces variance, whereas Gradient Boosting mainly reduces bias by adding up many weak learners, typically shallow trees.
Including both ensembles therefore allows us to compare two different ways of combining trees.
We tuned the learning rate, which scales the contribution of each tree, the number of trees and the maximum depth of each tree.
Because the trees have to be trained one after another, Gradient Boosting cannot be parallelized over the trees like Random Forest.
To limit the computation time, we therefore used a smaller grid and kept $\text{min\_df}$ at its default value.
The selected maximum depth was 1, meaning that each tree is a decision stump that splits on a single word.
This was combined with the highest learning rate (0.2) and the largest number of trees (300) in the grid.

In addition to accuracy, precision, recall and F1-score, we used McNemar's test \citep{Dietterich1998} to test whether difference in accuracy between the models is significant.
McNemar's test is computationally efficient and requires only a single train/test split, suiting our setup.
We compared Naive Bayes and Logistic Regression because they represent the generative and discriminative models, respectively \citep{Ng2001}.
The results of this test will indicate whether for this text classification task it is more effective to model the estimated generative probability of the words or to model the class boundary more directly.
We also compared Random Forest and Gradient Boosting against the Decision Tree baseline.
The results of these tests will indicate whether combining many trees into an ensemble significantly improves accuracy over a single tree.

We used the McNemar formula as follows:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mcnemar}
\chi^2 = \frac{\left(|n_{01} - n_{10}| - 1\right)^2}{n_{01} + n_{10}}
\end{equation}
This formula uses a $2 \times 2$ contingency table that counts the instances where the two models agree or disagree, where $n_{01}$ is the number of instances misclassified by the first model but not the second, and $n_{10}$ vice versa.
The null hypothesis states that both models perform with equal accuracy, while the alternative hypothesis states that the models differ in accuracy, tested at a significance level of $\alpha = 0.01$.

However, they show differences in precision and recall.
Naive Bayes has a higher recall (0.956) but lower precision (0.857), so it misses fewer AI-generated reviews but also incorrectly labels more human reviews as AI.

Both ensembles show the same pattern as Logistic Regression, with a lower recall (0.860 and 0.864) than precision (0.956 and 0.908), meaning they miss more AI-generated reviews than they wrongly flag human ones.

This means each word was approximately $e^{3.16} \approx 24$ to $e^{3.42} \approx 31$ times more likely to appear in AI-generated reviews.
These terms appears with relatively high frequency in the dataset (around 30 to 40 occurrences each), implying that their high occurrence in AI reviews was a primary driver for the Naive Bayes classification.
The words associated with AI-generation show formal characteristic, such as ``delight'' and ``notch,'', suggesting that GPT-4's output is more formal compared to human writing.
``Inconsis'' was also a top feature for AI, likely derived from ``inconsistent'' or ``inconsistently''. 
``Delight'' likely stems from ``delightful'' or ``delighted'', while ``notch'' likely from ``top-notch''.
Furthermore, ``inconsist'' and ``notch'' are primarily adjectives.
This suggests that GPT-4 relies on descriptive adjectives.
``Lastly'' is a distinct outlier in the list of words, since it is a transition word.
The use of it suggests that GPT-4 uses words that are structural connectors more frequently than humans.

The top five features for the Logistic Regression model were: \emph{delight}, \emph{lack}, \emph{offer}, \emph{stylish}, and \emph{notch}.
The two words \emph{delight} and \emph{notch} overlap between the two linear models, with \emph{delight} ranking highest for both.
The variance in word overlap can be attributed to Naive Bayes modeling the generative probability of individual words, whereas Logistic Regression models the class boundary.
\emph{Stylish} is an adjective, further suggesting that GPT-4 leans towards descriptive words.
Additionally, \emph{lack} was identified as a sign of AI-generation, suggesting that AI may focus more on missing services, whereas human reviews may do so less frequently.
While two AI-related words overlap between the linear models, none of the human words match.
This may be because AI produces more formulaic reviews, whereas human writing is more varied.

The features associated with AI generation appear to be more formal and polished in nature.
Furthermore, AI tends to describe the appearance of locations in polished terms, whereas humans focus more on actions (e.g. ``told,'' ``said,'' ``enter'').
This may suggest a difference in that AI describes experiences based on impressions of what is likely to occur, while humans describe their lived experiences.
One outlier ``com'' appears among the human features.
This is likely a fragment of ``booking.com'' as a result of data preprocessing. 
This resulted in Naive Bayes picking up on a platform-specific artifact of the human reviews, whereas AI does not mention the booking platforms.
A limitation of the linear models is the bag-of-words representation, which obscures the analysis of word order.



The top five features for the Decision Tree (\tableref{tab:top-features-trees}), ranked by Gini importance, are: \emph{hotel}, \emph{servic}, \emph{good}, \emph{central}, and \emph{staff}.
These features show no overlap with the linear models.
However, unlike the previous models, the AI-associated words are mainly about the hotel itself, such as \emph{hotel}, \emph{servic} (service), \emph{staff} and \emph{central}, likely because the tree favours common words that split many reviews.
For example, \emph{hotel} appears in 60\% of the AI-generated training reviews but only in 29\% of the human-written ones, which is consistent with the word frequency analysis in \sectionref{sec:data}.
\emph{Central} is rarer but more one-sided, appearing in 16\% of AI-generated and 3\% of human-written reviews.
The only exception is \emph{good}, which is associated with human reviews (28\% of human-written vs.\ 14\% of AI-generated reviews).
Although descriptive words are mostly associated with AI in the other models, \emph{good} points towards human reviews. This could be because AI uses more polished language (e.g., \emph{delight}, \emph{top-notch}) to appear convincing, whereas humans may use simpler, more neutral adjectives like \emph{good} to express satisfaction.

Random Forest and Gradient Boosting share exactly the same top five features, \emph{servic}, \emph{staff}, \emph{hotel}, \emph{room}, and \emph{lack}, only in a different order, and all of them point towards AI-generated reviews.
Three of these (\emph{hotel}, \emph{servic} and \emph{staff}) are also among the top features of the Decision Tree, suggesting that the ensembles rely on the same hotel-related vocabulary as the single tree.
In contrast to the Decision Tree, both ensembles rank \emph{lack} (in 14\% of AI-generated vs.\ 1\% of human-written reviews) among their top features.
\emph{Lack} is also the top AI feature of Logistic Regression, making it the only word shared between a tree-based and a linear model.
The importances of the ensembles are spread more evenly across the top words than those of the Decision Tree, since each word's importance is averaged over many trees.
For Gradient Boosting, the selected $\text{max\_depth}$ of 1 means that each tree is a single split on one word, so the model is an additive combination of individual word effects without interactions between words.

\subsection{Performance by Review Polarity}
\label{sec:polarity}

To investigate whether the selected Random Forest classifier performs better at distinguishing between fake and genuine reviews for positive versus negative sentiment, we evaluated its test set performance separately across positive and negative review subsets. 

As shown in \tableref{tab:polarity-rf}, Random Forest achieves slightly higher accuracy on negative reviews (0.9252) compared to positive reviews (0.8943), along with higher precision (0.9746 vs.\ 0.9346) and F1-score (0.9237 vs.\ 0.8850). To test whether this difference in accuracy is statistically significant, we performed a chi-square test on the contingency table of correct and incorrect classifications by sentiment polarity. The test yielded $\chi^2 = 1.1031$ ($p = 0.2936 > 0.01$), indicating that we fail to reject the null hypothesis. Consequently, the Random Forest model's ability to detect AI-generated reviews does not differ significantly between positive and negative reviews.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:sec:limitations-and-futurework}

The results obtained from an application perspective suggest that detecting AI-generated hotel reviews is a relevant task to identify fake or potentially misleading online content. 
However, several limitations need to be considered when interpreting the results.

First, this study was restricted to English hotel reviews. Although this allowed us to apply a consistent preprocessing pipeline, it limits the generalizability of our findings to other linguistic contexts. 
As online review platforms serve users worldwide, the expansion of AI-generated review detection to multiple languages represents an important direction for future research.
Therefore, multilingual models and machine translation could be explored to adapt the proposed approach to different languages. 
However, differences in grammar, syntax, and writing conventions may affect the affect the accuracy of the detection models. 

Second, the study focused exclusively on hotel reviews. However, the characteristics of AI-generated reviews may vary across domains, such as e-commerce, restaurants, and other service industries. 
Future research could therefore investigate whether the patterns identified in this study also occur in other types of reviews. 
Domain-specific models could be developed to account for differences in vocabulary, writing style, and review content. 
This step would help determine whether general or domain-specific models are more suitable.

Furthermore, the classification results may depend on the preprocessing techniques and model configurations. 
Future research could investigate the use of n-grams to capture recurring word combinations, alternative normalization techniques, and different tokenization methods. 
In addition, different classification models, including deep neural networks, could be explored to evaluate their suitability for AI-generated review detection. 
More extensive hyperparameter tuning could further optimize model configurations by systematically evaluating different parameter combinations.
